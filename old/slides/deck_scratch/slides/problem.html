The question: does this wafer have a scratch? A scratch is a thin, often curved line of failing dies from mechanical contact: a handling robot, a carrier or a polishing step. Two things make it worth catching. It repeats until the handler is fixed, and dies next to a scratch can pass test and still fail early in the field. So a flag does two things: it holds the handling path, and it sends the wafer to neighbour-die screening. In this dataset a wafer can carry several patterns at once, so the scratch has to be found among other defects, as in the right-hand map. Data: MixedWM38, 36,991 wafer maps after cleaning, Kaggle usability 7.5. The 49 percent scratch rate is artificial: the authors balanced the classes, partly with generated maps. The evaluation loss: a miss costs 10 times a false alarm, because a miss carries a repeating cost and a reliability cost while a false alarm is one inspection. That ratio is our assumption and we vary it later. Before any modelling we removed two things: 1,024 exact duplicate maps, almost all scratched, and the die count, which has no physical link to a scratch but takes values on 5 percent of clean wafers that no scratched wafer has.
